# Lesson plans and session plans

> Distilled from: k12-lesson-plan-creation and its subject references and output contract (anthropics/k12-teacher-skills, Apache-2.0); teach (mattpocock/skills, MIT); universal-diagnostic-tutor (SenmuuuuW/universal-diagnostic-tutor-skill, MIT).

A lesson plan is a script a real teacher can run tomorrow: phases with minutes that add up, the exact tasks learners do, what to look for while they work, and how to tell at the end who got it. Write it like a colleague's note, not an essay about pedagogy.

## 1. Clarify, then build

Ask at most 2 questions, in this priority: (1) learner level or grade, (2) topic or standard, (3) curriculum in use, (4) region or framework (state standards, national curriculum, exam board). Default everything else silently and state the defaults: a 45-60 minute period for school, mixed-ability class, universal access supports on.

Offer a quick draft before the full build when the lesson is new to the requester: one line naming level, topic and standard; a 3-sentence summary; one bullet per phase with minutes; the actual learner tasks; assumed prior knowledge; the exit ticket. Apply changes to the draft, then build the full plan and materials.

## 2. Ground it

- Anchor to a standard or objective. Quote the standard verbatim once (in the at-a-glance block); elsewhere refer to it by code plus a gist of ten words or fewer.
- When no standards database or curriculum source is available, say so in a footer: "Standards and misconceptions reflect general best practice." Never invent standard codes, curriculum citations or research.
- Copyright: write original tasks, texts and prompts. Use a named curriculum only to understand scope and structure; never reproduce its student-facing text. Don't name a curriculum's proprietary routines unless the teacher confirmed they use it.

## 3. Pick the lesson arc

| Situation | Arc (phases) |
|---|---|
| Maths, problem-based | Launch → Explore → Discuss → Synthesize → Exit ticket |
| Science, phenomenon-based | Launch phenomenon (notice and wonder, don't explain) → Investigation → Sensemaking discussion → Claim-evidence-reasoning (from grade 3) → Model update → Exit ticket |
| ELA, text-based | Launch → Read (with a purpose) → Text-dependent discussion → Write or word work → Exit ticket |
| Social studies, inquiry | Hook → Background (short, inline) → Source work (2 sources, 2-3 guided questions: observe → contextualise → corroborate) → Discussion → Exit ticket with evidence |
| Explicit instruction (new procedure, novices) | Review prior → Model (I do) → Guided practice (we do) → Independent practice (you do) → Exit ticket |
| University lecture | Retrieval warm-up → short explanation segments, each followed by an active task (problem, peer discussion, poll) → Synthesis → Minute paper or exit question |
| Corporate workshop block | Why it matters for your job → Demo on a real case → Practice on own work → Debrief → Commit to one next action |
| One-to-one or self-study lesson | One tightly scoped skill → short explanation → interactive practice with instant feedback → check → link to reference sheet |

Subject non-negotiables worth keeping:
- **Maths:** learners attempt before strategies are modelled; Discuss gets at least 10 minutes even in a short lesson (shrink other phases instead); a visual model on the page (tape diagram, number line, area model, ratio table, double number line, graph) matched to the concept.
- **Science:** investigation before explanation; three-dimensional targets stated separately (Science and Engineering Practice, Disciplinary Core Idea, Crosscutting Concept, names spelled out); models show mechanism, not decoration.
- **ELA:** 2-3 Tier 2 target words taught in context; questions answerable only from the text.
- **Social studies:** a compelling question that is contestable, matters to people, and is answerable with evidence ("When is civil disobedience justified?", not "When did X happen?"); real, specifically cited sources.

## 4. Section structure

1. **At a glance:** standard (verbatim, once), the arc in one line with minutes ("Launch 8 → Explore 15 → Discuss 12 → Synthesize 5 → Exit 5"), materials named plainly ("Number cards 0-20").
2. **Learning goal:** the big idea in one sentence; "learners will be able to" bullets naming sub-skills; prerequisite (prior standard or skill plus one sentence on what learners can already do).
3. **Vocabulary and anticipated challenges:** 3-5 terms with learner-friendly definitions, each taught in a named phase; 2-3 misconceptions as *What learners do* / *Why it happens* / *Teacher move*. A predicted error names one specific wrong answer.
4. **Lesson sequence:** each phase with minutes, the exact prompts, and for every work phase 3+ look-fors: the observable learner behaviour, why it matters, what to do about it. If the main task has more than one correct answer, one look-for says so. Every discussion phase names a learner-to-learner talk move (think-pair-share, turn-and-talk, agree/disagree) and gives specific prompts, never "discuss the answers".
5. **Exit ticket** with sort criteria (section 6).
6. **Design notes (last):** 2-3 elements to keep when adapting, including the central representation and one sentence on why. Rationale goes after the teaching path, not before.

## 5. Practice sets need structural variety

Before writing problems, list the topic's structural cases from the baseline case everyone must clear to the hardest case learners most often get wrong. Examples: start-unknown story problems in early maths; a product smaller than both factors when multiplying decimals; the missing-leg case for Pythagoras; linear-but-not-proportional relationships.

- Every listed case is a numbered, required problem (or the exit ticket). A case mentioned only in prose or teacher notes is not covered.
- Hard cases never replace the baseline case; an all-hard set fails like an all-easy one.
- The number domain counts: if the standard says rational numbers, at least one required problem uses fractions or decimals.
- Never park a required case in an optional extension.
- Say once which problem carries which case.

## 6. The exit ticket

- It is the hardest listed case, chosen with the misconception test: a learner who holds the lesson's main misconception must get it wrong. If they would get it right, swap it.
- Work it yourself and check the answer the problem actually produces.
- Three sort buckets with explicit criteria, not bare labels: *Got it* (e.g. "correct equation with the unknown where it lives in the story"), *Almost there*, *Needs re-teaching*. Equivalent forms of one answer sit in the same bucket.
- Science and ELA variants: a new small phenomenon to explain, or a text-dependent question needing evidence. More on checks in [checks-for-understanding.md](checks-for-understanding.md).

## 7. Everything must agree

- Phase minutes include transitions (handing out, regrouping, collecting) and sum exactly to the period.
- Materials list and phases match both ways; counted sets match ("Picture cards, 18" lists 18 words). Default kit is what every room has plus the pages you ship.
- A task worded in two places (plan and learner sheet) uses identical wording.
- Scripts and worked examples are final say-aloud text, every step decided.
- Every in-document reference exists ("the table below" is below).
- Verify every computation, answer key and quantity chain by working it.

## 8. Write for the reader

**Teacher pages:** paragraphs of 3 sentences or fewer; bullets as fragments of about 15 words; parallel variants (per group, per phase) in one table with short cells; at most one or two callouts per phase for the moments a teacher must not miss; framework names spelled out; sentence-case headings.

**Learner pages:** at the learners' reading level, which may differ from their grade ("grade-6 content in sentences a grade-3 reader can read"). Sentence length guide: about 8 words in K-2, about 12 in grades 3-5 with at most one subordinate clause, about 15 in grades 6-8; above that, length follows the content. One instruction at a time. Sentence starters where the writing move is hardest, never pre-filling the answer. Meaning never carried by colour or position alone ("the Evidence column", not "the box on the right"); every write-in space labelled; size the worksheet so a typical learner finishes in the minutes its phase allows.

Hand-outs as Word or PDF files: write the content here, then build the file with `docs-office` (`references/word-docx.md`, `references/pdf.md`).

## Checklist

- [ ] At most 2 questions asked; defaults stated; draft offered for a new lesson
- [ ] Standard quoted once; no invented codes or curriculum text
- [ ] Arc fits subject and setting; discussion has a talk move and real prompts
- [ ] Every structural case is a required problem; exit ticket is the hardest one and fails the misconception
- [ ] Minutes sum to the period; materials, wording and references agree
- [ ] Learner pages at reading level, accessible in black and white
