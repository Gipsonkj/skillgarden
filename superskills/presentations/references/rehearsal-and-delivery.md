# Rehearsal, delivery and Q&A

You learn what a talk is about by running it out loud against a clock, not by polishing slide four. Do the first timed run before the deck is finished; it tells you how much deck you actually need. Many figures below are practitioner defaults, not research; say so if the speaker asks "why that number", and let a timed run overrule them.

## 1. Diagnose first

When someone asks for help with a talk, find the real hurdle before giving tips:

| Symptom | Usual root cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Runs over time | Written to the material, not the slot | Accordion down (section 2); cut a whole pillar |
| Filler words, racing | Discomfort with silence under pressure | Practise pausing; the fillers fade with it |
| Monotone, stiff | Not trusting that emotion or a pause won't derail it | Energy games (section 5), a conversational persona |
| Freezes, loses place | Word-for-word script | Replace with cue notes and bookmarks (speaker-notes.md) |
| Weak endings | Attention returns near the finish line and the speaker lets go | Summary prompts: "So what I want you to remember is..." |
| Audience checks out at minute 5 | Explaining before convincing anyone there's a problem | Move the stakes into the first 3 minutes |
| Demo lands flat | Shown before the problem is believed, or unnarrated | Move it after its pillar; say what to watch first |
| Nerves | Normal adrenaline | Section 6 |

Ask also: does this need a presentation at all, or would an email do? Does the audience know whether you want a decision, feedback or just to inform them?

## 2. The accordion (find the talk, find the cuts)

1. Optional: dump every idea on paper.
2. Speak the talk to a **3-minute** timer. Stay in character, end strong, however rough. Note what you liked and didn't.
3. Repeat at **2 minutes**, **1 minute**, **30 seconds**.
4. Expand back up: 30 s, 1, 2, 3 minutes, adding material deliberately.
5. What survives the 30-second rep is the arrow and the pillars. What only reappears on the way up is the cut list, in cut order: let the rehearsal rank the cuts, not a guess.

About 15 minutes for a full accordion; works for a whole talk or one section.

## 3. Practice schedule

| Talk | Full runs | Rough time |
|---|---|---|
| Lightning (5 min) | 5-7 | 3 h |
| Conference (15 min) | 3-5 | 4-5 h |
| Seminar (45 min) | 3-4 | 6-8 h |
| Defense (60 min) | 4-6 | 10-15 h |

Progression: run 1 gets through it (it will run long; mark stumbles); run 2 smooths transitions and times each section; run 3 hits exact timing with the timer visible; run 4 is recorded on video and includes Q&A practice; later runs, the day before and the morning of, can be just the open and close.

- Speak aloud, standing, at real pace; silent read-throughs don't count.
- Measure the speaker's rate: read 200 of their own words aloud, time it, use that number to budget sections.
- Watch the recording for pace, fillers, looking down, and slides you talked over.
- Run it once for a colleague outside the team: they should be able to state the arrow back in one sentence, and mark where they got lost.
- Have 5-, 10- and full-length versions ready when the schedule is shaky.

**Quality gate before the day:** two consecutive timed runs inside the slot minus 10-15% buffer; every slide passes the one-idea and assertion-title tests; every pillar has evidence; any demo ends at least 2 minutes before the slot does.

## 4. Before you walk on

- Test on the actual projector or screen share: dark decks wash out in bright rooms; check the smallest text from the back.
- Export a PDF fallback and carry it (USB, cloud link); presentation software failing on someone else's machine is routine.
- Fonts embedded or installed on the venue machine; videos play offline; audio checked.
- Demo: zoom terminal and editor beforehand, notifications off, tabs closed, secrets purged, short shell prompt, seeded data, offline mode, and a recorded fallback on a slide.
- Presenter view working (notes, next slide, timer); clicker paired; phone on silent; water.
- Remote talks: camera at eye level, light in front, share one window not the desktop, close chat notifications, a second screen for notes.

## 5. Delivery

- **Pause.** Let a point land; silence reads as confidence. Pausing fixes fillers and racing at the root.
- **Think up.** When gathering a thought, look up and to the side, not down at the floor or notes.
- **End strong.** Land every point and the talk with a summary prompt ("So my point here is..."); the end is what people remember.
- **Stay in character.** Don't announce problems ("sorry, this slide is messy", "I'm nervous"); the audience can't see what you feel. If something breaks, keep going; they forget in seconds unless you stop and point at it.
- **Comment, don't read.** The slide names the point; you say why it matters, what it costs, what's surprising.
- **Narrate visuals.** Say what a chart shows before discussing it; the back row, captions and the recording depend on it. Narrate waits during demos.
- **Change state** every 3-5 slides or about 7 minutes: a question, a poll, a demo, a story, a guess-before-reveal. Online, more often (chat prompts, unmute, polls).
- **Energy leads, words follow.** Raise physical energy for conviction; conviction openers help: "I genuinely believe...", "This matters because...".
- **Humour without risk:** specific details (brand names, exact numbers), nostalgia ("we once faxed this form"), the list of two normal items and one surprising one; put the funny word last, stop talking, wait half a second. Self-deprecation is safe; jokes at others' expense are not.
- **Stories:** build them around one 5-second moment of realization; add stakes early (plant the problem, say your plan before executing it).
- Running behind: take the pre-decided skip from the notes, summarise instead of detailing, never rush or skip the conclusion. Ahead: expand an example or take a question; don't pad.

## 6. Nerves

- Reframe the adrenaline as readiness; it's the same physiology.
- Visualise the whole sequence: walking up, the first line landing, stepping off.
- Memorise the first line and the last; everything between is cue notes.
- "Dare to be dull": aim for connection, not perfection; it lowers self-monitoring and frees attention.
- Practise under disruption (a friend interrupts, random words to weave in, energy levels called out 1-10) so the real room feels calm by comparison.

## 7. Q&A

- Listen to the whole question; pause before answering; repeat or rephrase it for the room and the recording.
- **ADD:** Answer cleanly in one sentence, give a Detailed example, Describe why it matters to the asker.
- Keep answers to 30-90 seconds; offer to continue afterwards for deep dives or one person's edge case.
- Point to backup slides from the crib (speaker-notes.md) rather than improvising numbers.
- Don't know? Say so, say how you'd find out, and follow up.
- Hostile or critical: acknowledge the point, give your rationale, name the limitation honestly ("That's fair; we chose X because...; Y is worth testing"). Never argue or dismiss.
- If Q&A is inside your slot, plan 20-30% of it for questions and finish content early; if it is after, use the full slot and stop on time.
- Keep the final slide (links, contact, the ask) up during Q&A.

## 8. After the talk

- Note the questions you got; they become next time's crib and the slides you were missing.
- Judge it by whether people repeat the arrow in their own words, the session feedback, and traffic to the repo or docs; treat these as directional.
- On a published recording, the retention curve shows where it failed: the share still watching after 30 seconds judges the hook; dips locate the section that stopped paying. Fix that section of the outline; don't shorten the talk to chase a percentage.
- Store the arrow, pillars and cut list so a re-give in a different slot stays the same talk.

- [ ] Gate passed: two runs inside slot minus buffer
- [ ] Venue, fonts, fallback PDF, demo fallback checked
- [ ] Q&A crib rehearsed; backup slides numbered
