# Comments and engagement

> Distilled from: linkedin-engagement comment strategy (alirezarezvani/claude-skills, MIT), linkedin-marketing comment-drafter, reply-handler and voice rules (sergebulaev/linkedin-skills, MIT), linkedin-content-writer comment rules (microsoft/cat-agent-skills, MIT), linkedin-skills platform canon (alirezarezvani/claude-skills, MIT).

ToS reminder: comments, likes and replies are posted **by the user, by hand**. Automated commenting, liking or sharing, through an API or anything else, and engagement pods are prohibited under User Agreement section 8.2 ([tos-and-safe-automation.md](tos-and-safe-automation.md)).

## Why comments come first

From a standing start, your own posts reach almost nobody. A substantive comment on a post that already has an audience puts your name, your headline and a paragraph of your thinking in front of people already reading about your subject, for about 6 minutes of work.

## Drafting vs automating

Drafting a comment for the user to edit and post isn't automation. These are:
- auto-posting comments,
- generating batches of comments for posts the user hasn't read,
- pasting near-identical comments across many posts.

Rules for drafted comments:
- Draft only for posts the user has read and pasted (or described). Don't fetch threads with scrapers.
- The user adds or confirms the specific detail (their number, their case). A generic generated comment is easy to spot, and being spotted as someone who generates comments is worse than not commenting.
- Draft a handful a day at most, never the same text twice.

## What a comment competes on

It has to **add something the post didn't say**. Ways to do that:
1. A counter-example ("This holds until the team passes about 15 people...").
2. A number from the user's own experience.
3. The case where the idea breaks (the most valuable and the rarest).
4. A mechanism: why what the author saw happens.
5. A sharper question that only a careful reader could ask.

Agreement and restating the post don't count. "Great post", "This.", "100%" and emoji-only replies are invisible.

## Comment shapes

| Shape | Skeleton |
|---|---|
| Missing piece | "[Their thesis] misses one piece: [what moved]. When [condition], the real differentiator is [X], not [their focus]." |
| Answer their closing question | Direct answer in one sentence, then one example or number, then why it matters for their framing |
| Data first | "Half the [population] I see now [behaviour]. The old [assumption] broke around [date]." |
| Practitioner observation | "When [A] the system does X, when [B] it does Y. [The rule behind it]." |
| Counter with concession | "Agree on [point 1]. Where I'd push is [point 2], because [specific case]." |
| Sharper question | "The harder version of this is [deeper question]. Have you seen [specific case]?" |

Length: **200-350 characters** (about 25-50 words), up to 2 short paragraphs. Under about 12 words carries no weight. Over 500 characters looks like hijacking the thread. Introduce at least one idea the post didn't contain. Capitalise names. Don't name your own product on someone else's post; describe what you do instead. No links.

## Commenting roster

List 8-12 accounts the user would read anyway, scored on audience overlap and size tier:

| Tier | Size vs user | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Huge | 10x+ | Crowded (hundreds of comments). Less than half of any day's slots |
| Larger | 2-10x | Best ratio of reach to competition |
| Peer | About 1x | Reciprocity builds; at least one peer slot a day |
| Smaller | Under 0.5x | Goodwill, and some will grow |

Caps: no account more than **twice a week** (any more reads as following them around). Time budget is about 6 minutes per comment, so a 15-20 minute daily block gives 2-3 comments. Commenting earns attention; it doesn't buy it.

## Replies on the user's own posts

- Reply to every substantive comment within 24 hours, and to early ones within the first hour if possible (🟡: early replies correlate with reach; the exact "golden hour" is folklore).
- Reply with substance, not "thanks!". The reply is a second chance to make the point.
- Don't draft replies to generic praise, duplicates, spam or the user's own comments. Report the filter counts ("23 comments, 6 dropped: 4 praise, 1 duplicate, 1 spam, 17 drafted").
- Reply shapes: answer their question; concede then sharpen; extend their thesis; share a lived example with a caveat; ask back. 150-300 characters each.
- LinkedIn threads go only 2 levels deep. A reply to a reply attaches to the top-level comment.
- Never reuse the same reply template across a thread. Similar wording gets down-ranked and reads as automated.

## Engagement pods: refuse

Pods (groups that agree to comment on each other's posts on a schedule) are prohibited as inauthentic engagement, even when every click is manual. Third-party reports describe suppression after detection (🟡 drops like 8,500 to 340 impressions, 6-8 weeks to recover). The legitimate version is a **reciprocity list**: people whose work you genuinely read and comment on because you have something to say.

Never advise "react, wait 8-15 seconds, then comment so it looks human", "seeding" your own thread with extra comments, or timing tricks. That's advice on evading detection.

## Groups

Most LinkedIn Groups are dormant. Check whether the last 3 posts got real replies from named people. If not, skip the group. Off-LinkedIn communities (Slack, Discord, conferences) are often better, and what you learn there makes good posts.

## Measuring

There's no clean attribution. Watch, in order of reliability: profile views in weeks you commented consistently, connection requests from people you commented on, and people saying "I saw your comment on X". Treat commenting as a fixed habit, not a tracked channel.
